Dental Symptoms

“Doctor, Why Does My Tooth Hurt?”

Tooth pain is one of the most common reasons people decide to see a dentist. But “toothache” can mean many different things — from a brief sensitivity to pain caused by decay, inflammation, a cracked tooth or a problem around the gums.

By Dr. Anushka Choudhary · August 2026 · 6 min read
“Doctor, it only hurts sometimes. Do I really need to get it checked?”

This is an important question because the pattern of pain can provide clues — but the absence of pain does not always mean there is no dental problem.

What can tooth pain feel like?

Tooth pain does not always feel the same. You might experience a sharp “zing,” a dull ache, throbbing pain, discomfort when biting, or pain that appears after eating or drinking something hot or cold.

You may also notice that the pain is occasional, becomes more frequent over time, or is worse at night.

So, what can actually cause a toothache?

There are several possible causes, and the same type of pain can occur with different dental problems.

Some common possibilities include tooth decay, dental sensitivity, a cracked tooth, inflammation inside the tooth, gum problems, or problems around an existing filling or crown.

Pain can also sometimes be felt in one area even when the source is a nearby tooth or surrounding tissue. This is one reason a dental examination is useful when pain persists or keeps returning.

Tooth decay can cause pain

Tooth decay develops when acids produced by bacteria damage the tooth structure over time.

Early decay may not cause any symptoms at all. As the damage progresses, you may begin to notice sensitivity or pain, particularly with certain foods or drinks.

If decay reaches deeper parts of the tooth, the pain can become more persistent and may require more extensive treatment.

What if my tooth hurts when I bite?

Pain when biting or chewing can have several possible causes.

A tooth with decay, a crack, inflammation around the tooth, or an issue with an existing restoration can sometimes cause discomfort when pressure is applied.

If biting pain continues or keeps returning, it is worth having the tooth examined rather than simply avoiding chewing on that side.

What if cold drinks cause a sharp pain?

A brief sharp response to cold can occur with tooth sensitivity, particularly when dentine becomes exposed.

However, sensitivity can also occur in association with problems such as tooth wear, gum recession or tooth decay.

The duration and pattern of the pain matter. If the pain is severe, lingers after the stimulus is removed, or is becoming more frequent, a dental assessment is sensible.

Why does my tooth hurt more at night?

Some people notice tooth pain more strongly at night. This can happen for several reasons, including changes in how you perceive pain when you are resting and no longer distracted by daytime activity.

Night-time toothache can also occur with dental problems that require treatment, so repeated or significant night pain should not simply be masked with painkillers and ignored.

But what if the pain suddenly disappears?

This is where things can become confusing.

If a painful tooth suddenly stops hurting, it can be tempting to assume that the problem has fixed itself.

Feeling better does not always mean the underlying dental problem has disappeared.

Some dental conditions can change over time, and a reduction in pain does not by itself tell us what is happening inside or around the tooth.

If significant tooth pain has occurred and then suddenly disappears, especially if the tooth remains damaged or other symptoms are present, it is still a good idea to have the tooth assessed.

Can a cracked tooth cause pain?

Yes. A cracked tooth can sometimes cause pain when biting or chewing, and some people notice discomfort when they release their bite.

Cracks can be difficult to identify from symptoms alone, particularly when the pain comes and goes.

If you suspect that a tooth may be cracked, avoid repeatedly testing it by biting hard objects and arrange a dental assessment.

What about pain around the gums?

Not every dental ache comes directly from the tooth itself.

Gum inflammation, food trapped between teeth, or other problems around the gums can sometimes cause soreness or discomfort.

If the area is swollen, tender, bleeding frequently, or associated with bad breath or a loose tooth, it deserves professional attention.

When should you see a dentist?

You do not necessarily need to panic over every brief twinge. But persistent, recurrent or significant tooth pain should not be ignored.

Arrange a dental assessment if you notice:

• Tooth pain that keeps returning

• Pain that lasts or becomes more intense

• Pain when biting or chewing

• Swelling around the tooth or face

• Fever associated with dental pain

• A broken or cracked tooth

• A tooth that becomes unexpectedly loose

• Pain that interferes with sleep or normal activities

What can you do while waiting to see a dentist?

If you are waiting for an appointment, keep your oral hygiene routine gentle and consistent.

Continue brushing with a soft toothbrush and fluoride toothpaste. If food is trapped between the teeth, gentle interdental cleaning may help.

Avoid repeatedly exposing a painful tooth to very hot, very cold, sweet or hard foods if these trigger symptoms.

If you use an over-the-counter pain medicine, follow the product instructions and check that it is appropriate for you. Pain relief should not be used as a substitute for addressing the cause of persistent dental pain.

When is tooth pain an emergency?

Some symptoms require more urgent attention.

Seek urgent dental or medical care if tooth pain is accompanied by significant facial or neck swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or other severe symptoms.

One thing I wish more patients knew

I often hear people say: “It doesn't hurt anymore, so I left it.”

I understand why. When something stops hurting, it feels like the problem has gone away.

But teeth can be surprisingly good at becoming quiet while a problem still deserves attention.

Don't judge the health of a tooth only by whether it hurts today.

The takeaway

Tooth pain can have many possible causes, including decay, sensitivity, cracks, gum problems and inflammation.

The pattern of pain can provide useful clues, but it cannot reliably identify the cause by itself.

If pain is persistent, recurrent, worsening or associated with swelling or other concerning symptoms, arrange a dental examination.

And remember: feeling better does not always mean the tooth is better.
