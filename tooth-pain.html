Why Does My Tooth Hurt?

Tooth pain can range from a brief sharp sensation to a constant ache that makes it difficult to eat, sleep or concentrate. But what is actually causing it?

By Dr. Anushka Choudhary · August 2026 · 6 min read
“Doctor, my tooth hurts. Is it just sensitivity, or is something actually wrong?”

Tooth pain is a symptom rather than a diagnosis. Different dental problems can produce very similar sensations, which is why persistent pain deserves proper evaluation.

What can cause tooth pain?

There isn't one single reason why a tooth can hurt. The source may be the tooth itself, the tissues around it, the gums or even the jaw.

Some common possibilities include dental decay, tooth sensitivity, cracked teeth, gum problems, trauma and inflammation or infection involving the inside of the tooth.

  • Dental cavities or tooth decay
  • Tooth sensitivity
  • Cracked or fractured teeth
  • Gum inflammation or infection
  • Dental abscess or infection
  • Trauma or injury to a tooth
  • Problems involving the tissues inside the tooth

Is it sensitivity or something more?

Tooth sensitivity often feels like a short, sharp pain triggered by something such as a cold drink, ice cream, hot food or sometimes sweet or acidic foods.

The discomfort often disappears relatively quickly once the trigger is removed.

However, not every pain triggered by cold or heat is simply “sensitivity.” Cavities, cracks and inflammation inside a tooth can sometimes produce temperature-related pain too.

If you are unsure about the cause, don't try to diagnose the tooth based only on the sensation.

What does a cavity feel like?

Early tooth decay may not cause any noticeable symptoms. As decay progresses, however, a tooth may become sensitive to certain foods or drinks.

More advanced decay can cause pain, food getting stuck, sensitivity or discomfort when biting.

A cavity cannot be reliably diagnosed just by describing the pain. Your dentist may need to examine the tooth and, when appropriate, take dental X-rays.

Why does my tooth hurt when I bite?

Pain when biting can occur for several reasons.

A tooth with decay, a crack, inflammation around the root or other dental problems may become uncomfortable when pressure is applied.

If a previously comfortable tooth suddenly becomes painful when you bite, it is worth having it examined rather than repeatedly testing it yourself.

What if the pain is throbbing?

A throbbing or persistent toothache can occur when the tissues inside or around a tooth become significantly inflamed.

Sometimes the pain may become more noticeable when lying down or trying to sleep.

The pattern of pain can provide useful information to a dentist, but it cannot by itself establish a diagnosis.

Why does tooth pain sometimes become worse at night?

Many people notice dental pain more when they are trying to sleep. This can happen for several reasons, including changes in blood flow and pressure when lying down, but also because there are fewer distractions at night.

If tooth pain repeatedly wakes you from sleep, don't simply rely on painkillers to get through the night. Arrange a dental assessment to find out what is causing it.

My tooth stopped hurting. Does that mean it's better?

Not necessarily.

A reduction in pain does not automatically mean that the underlying dental problem has disappeared.

Dental pain can naturally fluctuate. Some problems may become temporarily less painful before returning.

In some situations, damage to the tissues inside a tooth can eventually result in reduced sensation even though the tooth still requires treatment.

This is why a tooth that previously caused significant pain should not automatically be considered “fixed” simply because it feels better.

What about a cracked tooth?

A cracked tooth can sometimes cause pain that is difficult to describe.

Some people notice discomfort when chewing or when releasing their bite. Others experience sensitivity to temperature.

Cracks can vary significantly in severity, so unexplained recurring pain associated with chewing deserves a dental examination.

Can gum problems cause tooth pain?

Yes.

Inflamed gums can become tender and uncomfortable around the teeth. Gum recession can also expose root surfaces, making teeth more sensitive.

If you also notice bleeding gums, swelling, persistent bad breath or gum recession, the problem may involve your gums rather than the tooth alone.

What if there is swelling?

Swelling around a painful tooth can be an important warning sign.

Dental infections can sometimes cause swelling in the gums, face or jaw.

A dental abscess is a collection of pus associated with an infection and requires professional dental treatment.

When should tooth pain be treated urgently?

Seek urgent dental or medical attention if tooth pain is accompanied by significant facial or jaw swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or rapidly worsening symptoms.

Fever associated with dental pain or swelling is also a reason to seek prompt professional assessment.

What can you do while waiting to see a dentist?

If you are waiting for a dental appointment, focus on keeping the area clean and avoiding anything that clearly triggers the pain.

You can continue gentle brushing with fluoride toothpaste. Avoid aggressively brushing a painful area.

If chewing causes pain, choosing softer foods temporarily may make eating more comfortable.

Over-the-counter pain medication may help some people, provided it is safe for them to take and used according to the product instructions or advice from a healthcare professional.

Pain medication should not be used as a substitute for identifying and treating the dental problem.

What will the dentist check?

Your dentist will usually begin by asking questions about when the pain started, what triggers it, how long it lasts and whether it has changed.

They may examine the tooth, surrounding gums and other structures in the mouth.

Depending on the situation, the dentist may perform specific clinical tests or recommend a dental X-ray or other imaging.

The goal is not simply to find where it hurts, but to identify why it hurts.

Does every toothache need a root canal?

No.

Tooth pain has many possible causes, and the appropriate treatment depends on the diagnosis.

Depending on the cause, treatment might involve improving oral hygiene, treating tooth decay, addressing gum disease, repairing a damaged tooth or other appropriate dental care.

Root canal treatment may be recommended for certain teeth when the pulp inside the tooth is irreversibly inflamed or infected, but it is not the treatment for every type of tooth pain.

One thing I wish more patients knew

Tooth pain is your body's way of telling you that something deserves attention.

You don't need to panic every time a tooth hurts. But repeatedly ignoring pain because it disappears can allow an underlying problem to progress.

The takeaway

Tooth pain can have many causes, ranging from relatively simple sensitivity to cavities, cracks, gum problems or inflammation and infection involving the tooth.

The type, timing and triggers of the pain can provide clues, but they cannot replace a dental examination.

If your pain is persistent, recurring, severe or associated with swelling, fever or other concerning symptoms, arrange a professional dental assessment.

And remember:

Feeling better doesn't always mean the problem is gone.
Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions About Tooth Pain

What are the most common causes of tooth pain?

Common causes include dental decay, sensitivity, cracked teeth, gum problems, trauma and inflammation or infection involving the tissues inside or around the tooth.

Why does my tooth hurt when I drink something cold?

Cold-triggered pain can occur with tooth sensitivity, enamel wear, gum recession, cavities or other dental problems. A dental examination can help determine the cause.

Why did my tooth pain suddenly stop?

Pain can fluctuate, and a decrease in pain does not necessarily mean the underlying problem has resolved. A previously painful tooth should still be assessed if the cause is unknown.

Should I see a dentist for tooth pain?

Persistent, recurring or severe tooth pain should be assessed by a dentist, particularly when accompanied by swelling, fever, pain on biting or other concerning symptoms.

Can tooth pain be an emergency?

Severe facial swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, rapidly worsening swelling or other serious symptoms require urgent professional care.
