Tooth Sensitivity

Why Do My Teeth Hurt When I Drink Something Cold?

A sudden sharp pain when you drink something cold can be surprisingly uncomfortable. But why does it happen? Here's what tooth sensitivity can mean — and when it deserves a closer look.

September 2026 · 6 min read · Dr. Anushka Choudhary, BDS

Have you ever taken a sip of an ice-cold drink and suddenly felt a sharp, quick pain in one or more teeth? If yes, you're not alone.

This is commonly described as tooth sensitivity, or dentin hypersensitivity. The pain is often sudden and short-lived, especially when the tooth comes into contact with something cold.

In simple terms: When the protective layers of a tooth become thinner or the gum moves away from the tooth surface, the underlying dentin can become exposed. Cold, hot, sweet or acidic foods and drinks may then trigger a brief painful response.

What exactly is tooth sensitivity?

The outer surface of the visible part of a tooth is covered by enamel. Underneath enamel is a layer called dentin. Dentin contains microscopic channels that connect toward the inner part of the tooth.

When dentin becomes exposed, external stimuli such as cold drinks can travel through these channels and trigger the nerves inside the tooth.

That's why the sensation can feel like a sudden “electric” or sharp twinge rather than a slow, dull toothache.

Why are my teeth sensitive to cold?

Several different things can lead to tooth sensitivity. Some are relatively simple, while others need dental treatment.

1. Enamel wear

Enamel protects the underlying dentin. Over time, excessive brushing, acidic foods and drinks, frequent snacking on acidic foods, or other forms of enamel wear can reduce this protective layer.

2. Gum recession

Tooth roots are not covered by enamel in the same way as the crown of a tooth. When gums recede, part of the root surface can become exposed, making the tooth more sensitive to cold.

3. Tooth decay

A cavity can weaken the tooth and expose areas that are normally protected. Sensitivity caused by decay may become more noticeable with cold, sweet or hot foods.

4. A cracked or damaged tooth

A crack in a tooth can sometimes cause sensitivity, especially when eating or drinking. Some people may also notice pain when biting or releasing their bite.

5. Recent dental treatment

Temporary sensitivity can occur after procedures such as fillings, dental cleaning or other dental treatments. In many cases this settles with time, but persistent or worsening symptoms should be checked.

6. Teeth grinding or clenching

Repeated grinding or clenching can contribute to tooth wear and may make teeth more sensitive.

Is sensitivity always a problem?

Not necessarily. Brief sensitivity that happens occasionally and disappears quickly may have a relatively simple explanation.

However, sensitivity should not simply be ignored if it is persistent, getting worse, severe, or limited to one particular tooth.

A dental examination can help determine whether there is an underlying problem such as decay, a crack, gum recession or another condition.

What can I do about sensitive teeth?

The right approach depends on the cause, but a few everyday habits can help protect your teeth.

  • Use a soft-bristled toothbrush and avoid aggressive horizontal brushing.
  • Consider a toothpaste formulated for sensitive teeth.
  • Limit frequent exposure to highly acidic foods and drinks.
  • If you have consumed something acidic, wait before brushing rather than brushing immediately.
  • Maintain good gum care and clean between your teeth.
  • Avoid extremely cold foods or drinks temporarily if they consistently trigger discomfort.

When should you see a dentist?

Make an appointment if your sensitivity is persistent, worsening or particularly painful.

You should especially get the tooth checked if:

  • The pain is coming from one specific tooth.
  • The pain lasts after the cold stimulus is gone.
  • You notice a visible hole, crack or damaged filling.
  • You have swelling or significant gum changes.
  • You experience pain while biting.
  • The sensitivity is interfering with eating or drinking.

How is tooth sensitivity treated?

Treatment depends on what is causing the sensitivity.

Sensitivity without significant underlying damage

Your dentist may recommend desensitising toothpaste, fluoride-based treatments or changes to your brushing technique and oral-care routine.

Gum recession or exposed roots

Treatment focuses on protecting the exposed area and managing the underlying gum problem where appropriate.

Tooth decay

If a cavity is responsible for the sensitivity, the decay needs to be treated rather than simply masking the symptom.

Cracked or damaged teeth

A cracked or structurally damaged tooth may require a restoration or other treatment depending on the extent of the damage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do my teeth hurt when I drink something cold?

Cold sensitivity can occur when dentin becomes exposed because of enamel wear, gum recession or other dental problems. Tooth decay and cracks can also cause similar symptoms.

Is tooth sensitivity normal?

Occasional brief sensitivity can happen, but persistent, worsening or severe sensitivity should be evaluated by a dentist.

Can sensitive teeth be treated?

Yes. Treatment depends on the cause and may include sensitivity toothpaste, fluoride treatment, improved brushing habits or treatment of decay, cracks or gum problems.

Should I stop drinking cold drinks?

You can temporarily reduce very cold foods and drinks if they trigger pain, but identifying the reason for the sensitivity is more important.

The bottom line

If cold drinks make your teeth hurt, it doesn't necessarily mean something is seriously wrong. But sensitivity is also not something you should have to simply live with.

The cause could be as simple as exposed dentin or brushing habits, but sensitivity can also be associated with decay, gum recession, cracks or other dental problems.

If the sensitivity keeps happening, becomes stronger, or affects one particular tooth, a dental examination is the best way to find out what's actually causing it.
