Dental Treatment Guide

Dental crowns,
explained simply.

A dental crown is a restoration that covers a tooth to help restore its shape, function and strength. Here's why a crown may be recommended, what happens during treatment and what you can expect afterward.

Understanding the treatment

What is a dental crown?

A dental crown is a custom-made restoration designed to cover a damaged, weakened or heavily restored tooth.

It fits over the visible portion of the tooth and is designed to restore its shape, function and appearance.

Crowns can be made from different materials. The appropriate choice depends on factors such as the tooth being treated, its location, appearance, function and the clinical situation.

Why might you need one?

Why are dental crowns recommended?

A crown may be considered when a tooth has lost a significant amount of structure and needs additional protection or support.

Crowns may be recommended for teeth that are significantly weakened by decay, large restorations, fractures or other forms of structural damage.

They may also be used in certain situations after root canal treatment, depending on the condition and remaining structure of the tooth.

A crown is not automatically required for every damaged or root-treated tooth. The decision depends on the individual tooth and its clinical condition.

An important point

A crown protects a tooth — it doesn't make a weak tooth "new."

A crown provides coverage and protection to the remaining tooth structure, but the natural tooth underneath still needs proper care. Good oral hygiene and regular dental examinations remain important.

Choosing the restoration

What are dental crowns made from?

Dental crowns can be made from different materials, and each material has its own characteristics.

Depending on the situation, crowns may be made from materials such as ceramic, porcelain-based materials, zirconia, metal or combinations of different materials.

The best option depends on factors such as the position of the tooth, appearance, chewing forces, available tooth structure and your individual dental needs.

Your dentist can explain which material is most suitable for your particular tooth.

During treatment

What happens when you get a crown?

1

Examination and planning

Your dentist examines the tooth and determines whether a crown is appropriate. The tooth may also require other treatment before it can be restored.

2

Local anaesthesia

Local anaesthesia is generally used during tooth preparation to make the procedure comfortable.

3

Tooth preparation

The tooth is carefully prepared to create the appropriate space and shape needed for the crown.

4

Impression or digital scan

An impression or digital scan may be taken so the crown can be designed to fit the prepared tooth and surrounding teeth.

5

Temporary crown

Depending on the treatment process, a temporary restoration may be placed while the final crown is being made.

6

Final crown placement

The final crown is checked for fit, appearance and bite before it is permanently cemented or otherwise secured when appropriate.

A common concern

Does getting a crown hurt?

The tooth is generally numbed with local anaesthesia during preparation, so the procedure is intended to be comfortable.

You may experience pressure, vibration or movement while the tooth is being prepared.

Some sensitivity or gum tenderness may occur after the appointment. If you experience significant, persistent or worsening pain, contact your dentist so the tooth can be assessed.

After treatment

What can you expect afterward?

Some temporary sensitivity or gum tenderness can occur following crown treatment.

Your dentist may give you specific instructions regarding eating, cleaning and caring for the tooth, particularly while a temporary crown is in place.

Once the final crown is placed, it should be cared for like your other teeth with regular brushing and cleaning between the teeth.

Regular dental check-ups are also important so that the crown, the underlying tooth and the surrounding gums can be monitored.

Protecting your crown

How should you care for a crowned tooth?

A crowned tooth still needs daily care. Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and clean between your teeth regularly.

Avoid using your teeth to bite extremely hard objects or non-food items, as excessive forces can damage both the crown and the underlying tooth.

If you notice that the crown feels loose, your bite feels different, or you develop pain or sensitivity, arrange a dental review.

Common questions

Dental crowns: quick answers

Does every root canal-treated tooth need a crown?

Not necessarily. The need for a crown depends on factors such as the amount of remaining tooth structure, the tooth involved and the forces it experiences. Your dentist can assess whether additional protection is appropriate.

How long does a dental crown last?

The lifespan of a crown varies depending on factors such as the material, oral hygiene, bite forces, tooth condition and general care. Regular dental examinations can help identify problems early.

Can a crowned tooth get a cavity?

Yes. A crown itself cannot develop tooth decay, but the natural tooth underneath or around the crown can still develop decay, particularly if plaque accumulates around the crown margins.

Can a crown come off?

A crown can occasionally become loose or dislodged. If this happens, avoid trying to permanently fix it yourself and contact your dentist for assessment.

Can a crown be replaced?

Yes. Crowns may eventually need replacement because of wear, damage, changes in the underlying tooth or problems around the restoration. Your dentist can determine whether replacement is necessary.

Can I eat normally after getting a crown?

Your dentist will advise you based on whether you have received a temporary or final crown. If a temporary crown is present, you may be given specific instructions about foods and chewing on that side.

Remember

A crown is only one part of protecting a tooth.

Even after receiving a crown, maintaining good oral hygiene, managing your diet, avoiding damaging habits and attending regular dental examinations remain important for the health of the tooth and surrounding tissues.
