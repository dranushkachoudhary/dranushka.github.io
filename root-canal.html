Dental Treatment Explained

Root Canal Treatment,
explained simply.

A root canal can sound intimidating. But what actually happens inside the tooth, why might treatment be needed, and does it really hurt?

Dentistry · Patient Education · August 2026

What is a root canal treatment?

Root canal treatment, also called endodontic treatment, is a procedure used to treat problems affecting the soft tissue inside a tooth.

Inside each tooth is a space containing the dental pulp, which contains nerves and blood vessels. If this tissue becomes severely inflamed or infected and cannot recover, root canal treatment may be recommended to treat the inside of the tooth while allowing the natural tooth to remain in place.

The goal of a root canal is usually not to remove the whole tooth — it is to treat the inside of the tooth so the natural tooth can be preserved.

Why might a tooth need a root canal?

There are several situations in which the pulp inside a tooth can become inflamed or infected.

  • Deep tooth decay reaching the pulp
  • A crack or fracture that affects the inside of the tooth
  • Significant trauma to a tooth
  • Repeated dental procedures on the same tooth
  • Other damage that causes irreversible inflammation or infection of the pulp

What symptoms might you notice?

A tooth that needs root canal treatment may cause symptoms, but the pattern can vary from person to person.

  • Persistent or severe tooth pain
  • Pain when biting or chewing
  • Prolonged sensitivity to hot or cold
  • Swelling around the tooth or gums
  • A pimple or drainage near the gum
  • Darkening or discoloration of a tooth in some situations

Importantly, the absence of pain does not always mean that the tooth is healthy. Some teeth with significant problems may have few or no symptoms.

What happens during a root canal?

The exact process can vary depending on the tooth and the clinical situation, but root canal treatment generally involves several stages.

1. Examination and diagnosis

Your dentist examines the tooth and may use dental X-rays and other tests to understand the condition of the tooth and surrounding tissues.

2. Local anaesthesia

Local anaesthesia is normally used to numb the area so the procedure can be performed comfortably.

3. Accessing the inside of the tooth

A small opening is made in the tooth to access the pulp chamber and root canal system.

4. Cleaning and shaping

The diseased or infected tissue is removed and the root canal system is carefully cleaned and shaped.

5. Filling and sealing

Once the canals have been appropriately cleaned, they are filled and sealed to help prevent further contamination.

6. Restoring the tooth

The tooth then needs a suitable final restoration. Depending on the amount of tooth structure remaining, this may include a filling or a crown.

Does a root canal hurt?

This is one of the most common questions people have.

With appropriate local anaesthesia, the procedure itself should generally be comfortable. You may feel pressure, vibration or movement, but you should tell your dentist if you experience pain during treatment.

The tooth or surrounding area can sometimes feel tender after treatment. This usually settles, but persistent, severe or worsening symptoms should be discussed with your dentist.

What happens after a root canal?

After the root canal has been completed, the tooth needs to be restored appropriately. This is important because a tooth that has undergone root canal treatment may need additional protection depending on how much tooth structure remains.

Your dentist will advise you about the appropriate restoration and whether a crown or another type of restoration is recommended.

Can a root canal save the tooth?

In suitable cases, yes. One of the major purposes of root canal treatment is to allow a diseased or infected tooth to remain in the mouth rather than removing it.

However, whether a tooth can be successfully treated depends on factors such as the amount of remaining tooth structure, the condition of the surrounding tissues and the overall restorability of the tooth.

Common misconceptions about root canals

“Root canals are extremely painful.”

Modern dental treatment uses local anaesthesia and techniques designed to keep patients comfortable. The pain caused by an untreated dental infection can sometimes be more significant than the procedure itself.

“If the pain disappears, I don't need treatment.”

Not necessarily. In some situations, symptoms can change or disappear even though the underlying problem remains. A proper dental examination is needed to determine what is actually happening.

“A root canal means the tooth is dead.”

A root canal removes the diseased pulp tissue from inside the tooth. The tooth can still remain functional in the mouth because it continues to be supported by the surrounding tissues.

When should you see a dentist?

Consider arranging a dental examination if you have persistent tooth pain, prolonged sensitivity, pain while biting, swelling, a recurring gum pimple near a tooth, or other symptoms that concern you.

If you develop significant facial swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or rapidly worsening symptoms, seek urgent professional medical or dental care.

Common Questions

Root canal FAQs.

How long does a root canal take?

Treatment time varies depending on the tooth, the complexity of the root canal system and the clinical situation. Some treatments can be completed in one appointment, while others may require more than one visit.

Is a crown always needed after a root canal?

Not every tooth automatically requires the same restoration. The decision depends on factors including which tooth was treated, how much tooth structure remains and the amount of functional stress the tooth is expected to handle.

Can a root canal fail?

Root canal treatment has a high success rate in appropriate cases, but no dental procedure can guarantee success. Persistent infection, new decay, cracks or other problems can affect a treated tooth and may require further assessment.

Is it better to extract the tooth instead?

Extraction and replacement may be appropriate in some cases, but if a natural tooth can be predictably restored and maintained, preserving it may be considered. Your dentist can discuss the options based on your individual situation.
