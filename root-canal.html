Dental Treatment Explained

Root Canal Treatment,
explained simply.

A root canal doesn't have to be mysterious or frightening. Learn why a tooth may need root canal treatment, what happens during the procedure, whether it hurts and how the tooth is cared for afterward.

Dentistry · Patient Education · August 2026

What is a root canal treatment?

Root canal treatment, also called endodontic treatment, is a dental procedure used to treat disease or damage affecting the pulp inside a tooth.

The pulp contains nerves and blood vessels and is located in the centre of the tooth. If the pulp becomes irreversibly inflamed or infected, treatment may be needed to remove the affected tissue, clean the root canal system and seal the tooth.

The purpose of a root canal is usually to treat the inside of the tooth while preserving the natural tooth in the mouth.

Why might a tooth need a root canal?

The pulp inside a tooth can become damaged for several reasons. Common causes include:

  • Deep tooth decay reaching the pulp
  • A crack or fracture affecting the tooth
  • Significant trauma or injury
  • Repeated procedures on the same tooth
  • Other damage causing irreversible inflammation or infection of the pulp

What symptoms might you notice?

A tooth requiring root canal treatment may produce noticeable symptoms, although not everyone experiences pain.

  • Persistent or severe toothache
  • Pain when biting or chewing
  • Sensitivity to hot or cold that lingers
  • Swelling around the tooth or gums
  • A recurring pimple or drainage near the gum
  • Discolouration or darkening of a tooth in some cases

One important point: pain disappearing does not always mean that the problem has disappeared. Some teeth with significant pulp or infection-related problems may have few or no symptoms.

What happens during a root canal?

The exact procedure depends on the tooth and the individual clinical situation, but root canal treatment generally involves several stages.

1. Examination and diagnosis

Your dentist examines the tooth and surrounding tissues. X-rays and other diagnostic tests may be used to determine the condition of the tooth and help plan treatment.

2. Local anaesthesia

Local anaesthesia is used to numb the area so the procedure can be performed as comfortably as possible.

3. Accessing the tooth

A small opening is made in the tooth to provide access to the pulp chamber and root canal system.

4. Cleaning and shaping

Diseased or infected pulp tissue is removed. The root canals are then cleaned and shaped to prepare them for filling.

5. Filling and sealing

After appropriate cleaning, the canals are filled and sealed to help prevent bacteria from entering the treated space again.

6. Restoring the tooth

The tooth is restored to protect it and return it to function. Depending on the tooth and the amount of remaining structure, this may involve a filling, crown or another suitable restoration.

Does a root canal hurt?

This is probably the biggest concern people have when they hear the words “root canal.”

With appropriate local anaesthesia, the procedure itself should generally be comfortable. You may notice pressure, vibration or movement during treatment, but you should tell your dentist if you experience pain.

Some tenderness or discomfort can occur after treatment while the surrounding tissues settle. Your dentist can advise you on appropriate aftercare.

A root canal is performed to treat a painful or diseased tooth — it is not intended to make the problem worse.

What happens after a root canal?

Completing the root canal is only part of the treatment. The tooth usually needs an appropriate final restoration to protect it and restore its function.

Depending on the tooth and how much healthy tooth structure remains, your dentist may recommend a filling, crown or another restoration.

Until the tooth has been appropriately restored, your dentist may advise you to avoid putting excessive pressure on it.

Can a root canal save the natural tooth?

In many suitable cases, root canal treatment can allow a natural tooth to remain in the mouth rather than being extracted.

However, not every tooth is restorable. The decision depends on factors such as the amount of remaining tooth structure, the condition of the surrounding tissues, the presence of cracks and the overall prognosis of the tooth.

Common misconceptions about root canals

“Root canals are extremely painful.”

Modern root canal treatment is performed using local anaesthesia and techniques designed to maintain patient comfort. An untreated dental infection or inflamed pulp can itself cause significant pain.

“My tooth stopped hurting, so I don't need treatment.”

Not necessarily. Symptoms can change even when the underlying dental problem remains. A dental examination is needed to determine whether treatment is still required.

“A root canal means the tooth is dead.”

Root canal treatment removes the diseased pulp from inside the tooth. The tooth can still function in the mouth because it remains supported by the surrounding tissues.

“Extraction is always better than a root canal.”

Extraction may be necessary when a tooth cannot be predictably restored. However, when a natural tooth can be successfully treated and maintained, preserving it may be an option worth considering.

When should you see a dentist?

Consider arranging a dental examination if you experience persistent tooth pain, prolonged sensitivity, pain while biting or chewing, swelling, a recurring gum pimple or any other symptom that concerns you.

Do not rely only on pain to decide whether something is wrong. Some dental infections or pulp problems may produce limited symptoms.

If you develop significant facial swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or rapidly worsening symptoms, seek urgent professional dental or medical care.

How can you protect a root-treated tooth?

A root-treated tooth still needs regular care. Good brushing, cleaning between the teeth and routine dental examinations are important for maintaining the tooth and the surrounding gums.

The final restoration also plays an important role. Follow your dentist's advice about the restoration, cleaning and review schedule recommended for your tooth.

Common Questions

Root canal FAQs.

How long does a root canal take?

Treatment time varies depending on the tooth, the number and shape of the canals, the complexity of the case and the treatment plan. Some cases can be completed in one appointment, while others may require more than one visit.

Is a crown always needed after a root canal?

Not every tooth automatically requires the same type of restoration. The decision depends on the tooth involved, how much structure remains and how much functional stress the tooth is expected to withstand.

Can a root canal fail?

Root canal treatment can be successful in appropriate cases, but no dental procedure guarantees success. Persistent infection, new decay, cracks or other complications can affect a treated tooth and may require further assessment.

Can I eat normally after a root canal?

Your dentist will advise you based on the stage of treatment and the restoration placed. If the tooth has not yet received its final restoration, you may be advised to avoid chewing hard foods on that tooth.

Can a tooth need another root canal?

In some situations, a previously treated tooth may develop persistent or recurrent problems. Depending on the cause, further treatment such as retreatment or another procedure may be considered after an appropriate evaluation.
