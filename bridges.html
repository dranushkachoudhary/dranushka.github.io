Tooth Replacement Guide

Dental bridges,
explained simply.

A dental bridge is one option for replacing a missing tooth or several missing teeth. Here's how bridges work, when they may be considered and what you can expect from treatment.

Dental bridge — click the image to view it in full size.

Understanding the treatment

What is a dental bridge?

A dental bridge is a fixed dental restoration used to replace one or more missing teeth.

The replacement tooth or teeth are supported by structures connected to neighbouring natural teeth, dental implants or, in some designs, other supporting structures.

Unlike a removable denture, a conventional fixed bridge is designed to stay in place during normal daily use.

Why might you need one?

Why are dental bridges recommended?

Losing a tooth can affect chewing, appearance and the way neighbouring teeth contact each other.

Depending on the location of the missing tooth and the condition of the surrounding tissues, a bridge may be considered as one way to restore the missing space.

However, a bridge is not automatically the best choice for every missing tooth. Your dentist needs to assess the individual situation first.

An important point

A dental bridge is one option — not the only option.

Depending on the number and position of missing teeth, the condition of neighbouring teeth and gums, bone support and your overall oral health, treatment options may include a bridge, dental implant or removable partial denture.

Different designs

What are the different types of bridges?

Traditional bridge

A traditional bridge is supported by crowns placed on suitable teeth on either side of the space.

Cantilever bridge

A cantilever bridge is supported from one side rather than both sides. Whether it is suitable depends on factors such as the location of the missing tooth and the forces placed on the restoration.

Maryland bridge

A Maryland bridge uses a framework or wings that attach to the back surfaces of supporting teeth. It may be considered in selected situations.

Implant-supported bridge

An implant-supported bridge uses dental implants as support rather than relying entirely on natural teeth.

During treatment

What happens when getting a dental bridge?

1

Examination and planning

Your dentist examines the missing tooth area and evaluates the neighbouring teeth, gums, bite and other factors that may affect the treatment.

2

Preparing the supporting teeth

If a conventional bridge is planned, the supporting teeth may need to be carefully prepared to create space for the crowns.

3

Impression or digital scan

An impression or digital scan may be taken to help design the bridge accurately.

4

Temporary restoration

Depending on the treatment plan, a temporary restoration may be placed while the final bridge is being prepared.

5

Final placement

The final bridge is checked for fit, bite, comfort and appearance before being placed appropriately.

A common concern

Does getting a dental bridge hurt?

If natural teeth need to be prepared for a conventional bridge, local anaesthesia is generally used to make the procedure more comfortable.

You may experience pressure, vibration or some temporary sensitivity around the treated teeth.

If significant or persistent pain develops, contact your dentist so the area can be examined.

After treatment

What can you expect afterward?

Some temporary sensitivity or gum tenderness may occur after bridge preparation or placement.

Your dentist may give you specific instructions about eating, cleaning and caring for the restoration.

It may take a short period of time to become accustomed to the new restoration, particularly when chewing.

Long-term care

How do you care for a dental bridge?

A bridge needs regular cleaning just like natural teeth. Brush twice a day and clean between the teeth and around the bridge.

Depending on the bridge design, your dentist or hygienist may recommend floss threaders, interdental brushes or other cleaning aids to clean underneath the replacement tooth.

Regular dental examinations are also important because the supporting teeth and surrounding gums need to remain healthy.

Remember

The supporting teeth matter just as much as the bridge.

A bridge depends on healthy supporting structures. Maintaining good oral hygiene and attending regular dental check-ups can help protect the teeth and gums supporting the restoration.

Common questions

Dental bridges: quick answers

How long does a dental bridge last?

The lifespan of a bridge varies depending on factors such as the material, oral hygiene, bite forces, supporting teeth and general care. Regular dental check-ups can help identify problems early.

Can a dental bridge come loose?

A bridge can occasionally become loose or dislodged. If this happens, contact your dentist for assessment rather than attempting a permanent repair yourself.

Can you get a cavity under a dental bridge?

The natural teeth supporting a bridge can still develop decay, especially around areas where plaque accumulates. Good cleaning and regular dental care remain important.

Is a bridge better than a dental implant?

Neither option is automatically better for every patient. The appropriate choice depends on the condition of the surrounding teeth and gums, bone support, overall oral health, treatment goals and other individual factors.

Can I eat normally with a dental bridge?

A properly fitted bridge is designed to restore chewing function. Your dentist may recommend temporary dietary precautions while you adjust to the restoration.

Can a bridge replace more than one tooth?

Yes, some bridges can replace more than one missing tooth. Whether this is appropriate depends on the location of the missing teeth and the supporting structures available.

Final thought

Replacing a missing tooth is about more than appearance.

Restoring a missing tooth can help with chewing and function, but the best treatment depends on your individual oral health. A proper dental examination can help you understand which replacement options are suitable for you.
