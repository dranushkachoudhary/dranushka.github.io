Oral Health

“Doctor, Why Do I Have Bad Breath?”

Bad breath is common, but persistent bad breath can sometimes be a sign that something in your mouth needs more attention.

By Dr. Anushka Choudhary · August 2026 · 5 min read
“Doctor, I brush every day. Why do I still have bad breath?”

This is a very common question — and the answer isn't always simply “you aren't brushing enough.”

Bad breath can have several causes, and finding the cause is more useful than simply trying to cover the smell.

First: what actually causes bad breath?

Bad breath, also called halitosis, can develop when odour-producing substances accumulate in the mouth.

Bacteria can break down food particles, plaque and other substances in the mouth and produce compounds that have unpleasant smells.

This is why the condition of your teeth, gums, tongue and overall oral hygiene can play an important role.

Could your tongue be causing the smell?

Your tongue has a textured surface where bacteria and debris can accumulate.

In some people, this buildup can contribute significantly to bad breath.

Cleaning the tongue gently as part of your daily oral hygiene routine may help reduce this buildup.

Don't just focus on your teeth. Your tongue is part of your oral hygiene routine too.

What about plaque and gum disease?

Another important cause of persistent bad breath is gum disease.

Plaque that remains around the teeth and gums can contribute to inflammation.

Gingivitis may cause symptoms such as red, swollen or bleeding gums, while more advanced periodontal disease can involve deeper infection and damage to the tissues supporting the teeth.

If bad breath occurs together with bleeding or swollen gums, it is worth having your gums assessed by a dentist.

Could food be responsible?

Certain foods can temporarily affect the smell of your breath.

Garlic, onions and some strongly flavoured foods contain compounds that can contribute to noticeable breath odour.

This type of bad breath often improves as the food is digested and the compounds leave the body.

However, if bad breath continues regularly regardless of what you eat, there may be another underlying cause.

What about dry mouth?

Saliva plays an important role in keeping the mouth clean.

It helps wash away food particles and contributes to the natural cleaning of the mouth.

When saliva production decreases, bacteria and debris can accumulate more easily.

This can contribute to bad breath.

Dry mouth can happen for several reasons, including dehydration, certain medications and some medical conditions.

Can not drinking enough water affect your breath?

Yes, dehydration can contribute to a dry mouth.

If your mouth feels unusually dry, drinking enough water throughout the day can be a simple part of maintaining normal oral function.

However, persistent dry mouth should not simply be ignored, especially if it is accompanied by ongoing bad breath or other oral symptoms.

Does brushing more often fix bad breath?

Not necessarily.

Good oral hygiene is important, but aggressively brushing your teeth repeatedly is not the solution.

Instead, focus on thorough but gentle cleaning.

  • Brush your teeth twice a day with fluoride toothpaste.
  • Clean between your teeth regularly using floss or another appropriate interdental cleaner.
  • Gently clean your tongue.
  • Drink enough water and avoid allowing your mouth to remain persistently dry.
  • Have regular dental examinations when appropriate.

What if I use mouthwash?

Mouthwash may help temporarily reduce breath odour, depending on the type of product.

But mouthwash should not be used simply to mask persistent bad breath without investigating why it is happening.

If the underlying problem is plaque accumulation, gum disease, tooth decay or another oral condition, addressing that problem is more important than simply covering the smell.

Could a cavity cause bad breath?

Dental decay can sometimes contribute to unpleasant odour, particularly when food and bacteria become trapped in areas that are difficult to clean.

If bad breath is accompanied by tooth pain, sensitivity, visible holes or food repeatedly getting stuck around a tooth, a dental examination can help determine the cause.

Is bad breath always caused by the mouth?

No.

Most persistent bad breath is related to the mouth, but other factors can sometimes contribute.

Certain medications, dry mouth and some medical conditions may influence breath odour.

If a dental examination does not identify an oral cause, your dentist may recommend discussing persistent symptoms with an appropriate healthcare professional.

When should you see a dentist?

If your bad breath persists despite maintaining good oral hygiene, it is reasonable to have your mouth checked.

You should be particularly attentive if bad breath occurs together with:

• Bleeding or swollen gums

• Persistent gum tenderness

• Tooth pain or sensitivity

• Loose teeth

• Pus around the gums

• A persistent unpleasant taste

These symptoms can have different causes, so an examination is more useful than trying to diagnose the problem from symptoms alone.

What will your dentist check?

Your dentist may examine your teeth, gums, tongue and other areas of the mouth to look for possible sources of the odour.

They may check for plaque, calculus, gum inflammation, periodontal problems, tooth decay, infections or other oral conditions.

The treatment depends on what is causing the problem.

What can you do today?

Start with the basics.

Brush twice a day using fluoride toothpaste and clean between your teeth regularly.

Gently clean your tongue and stay adequately hydrated.

If you wear dentures or other removable oral appliances, follow the cleaning instructions provided for them.

And if the problem continues despite these measures, don't simply keep buying products to cover the smell.

Persistent bad breath is a symptom worth investigating, not something you simply have to live with.

One thing I wish more patients knew

Many people feel embarrassed about bad breath and avoid talking about it.

But dentists hear this concern all the time.

You don't need to feel embarrassed about asking for help.

The important question isn't simply: “How can I make the smell disappear?”

It is: “Why is it happening?”

The takeaway

Bad breath is common and can have many causes.

Oral hygiene, plaque buildup, tongue bacteria, gum disease, dry mouth, food and dental problems can all contribute.

Good daily oral hygiene can help, but persistent bad breath deserves professional assessment rather than simply being covered with mints or mouthwash.

If your breath has remained noticeably unpleasant despite consistent oral care, consider scheduling a dental examination.

Your breath can sometimes tell you that your mouth needs a little more attention.
